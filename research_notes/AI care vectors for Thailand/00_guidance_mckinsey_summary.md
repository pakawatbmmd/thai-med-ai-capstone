# Guidance document: summary of "The AI-powered future of care" (McKinsey, Sept 2026)

The student supplied this as the guiding frame for council v6. This is our own summary; the PDF itself is not committed because the repo is public and the document is copyrighted.

**Authors:** Aaron Lee, Jack Eastburn, Jessica Lamb, Jordan VanLare (McKinsey Healthcare Practice), September 2026. 14 pages, US-focused.

## Core frame: three vectors of AI's impact on healthcare
1. **Discursive care.** The reasoning and dialogue parts of care: intake, triage, diagnosis, guidance, referrals, follow-up. "Clinical interactions that do not require hands-on physical examination or treatment." Already emerging; this is the article's focus.
2. **Personalized and optimized care pathways.** AI trained on large volumes of individual history and outcomes data (clinical, claims, genomics) picks the best treatment for each individual.
3. **Autonomous interventions.** AI plus robotics, devices and software in closed loops that *deliver* care, not just plan it.

The three develop in parallel, and none is a prerequisite for another.

## Key numbers (US, 2024 claims; Merative, CMS)
- About 1 in 3 US adults use AI chatbots for health information at least monthly (KFF, Jun 2026).
- **16–22% of US outpatient claims by volume** (about 2–3 billion claims), and **13–19% of outpatient spending**, are for care elements discursive AI can already perform:
  - E&M-only or low-complexity E&M visits: 11–15% of claims.
  - Diagnostic and imaging *interpretation*: 5–7% of claims.
- AI-performable share by site of care: virtual 34%, emergency/urgent care 32%, office 22%, hospital outpatient 22%.
- **Workforce:** AI can perform a substantial share of core tasks for **11 of 19 million US healthcare workers (~60%)**. Share of core tasks AI can do: physicians 50%, social workers 53%, RNs 40%, pharmacists 36%, NPs 38% (O*NET task analysis, BLS 2024).
- **Access gap:** 92.3M Americans live in primary-care shortage areas. The average wait for an appointment is 31 days. 36% of adults skipped care because of cost. Among adults using AI for care, 27% cite cost, 21% feeling dismissed, and 16% lack of access.

## "Signals of scale" (how to tell discursive care is becoming a pillar)
- Primary and urgent care visits augmented with AI (AI-first encounters with clinician oversight or escalation).
- Visits diverted to AI (episodes fully resolved without a physician appointment).
- Front-door AI deployed by health systems, academic medical centers and national payers.
- Payer and clinician acceptance of closed-loop referrals from AI clinicians.
- Dedicated ICD/CPT billing codes for AI services (interpretation, triage, care management).
- Regulatory boundary expansion: states letting AI make clinical decisions without a physician.

## Consequences and themes
- **Payments:** fee-for-service providers lose low-acuity volume. Value-based providers gain. **Payers gain most and may shift "from financing care to orchestrating it"**, with a payer-deployed AI clinician as the patient's primary interface.
- **Care cascades:** more primary-care access risks low-value cascading care (94.4% of physicians have seen cascades after incidental findings). AI could break the cascades.
- **Workforce:** AI-first care. Clinicians move to oversight, escalation and complex judgement. Two challenges: workflow redesign, and **deskilling** (organisations must "actively design against" it with supervised unassisted practice).
- **Quality:** less error (an "AI panel of world-class specialists" behind every visit; MAI-DxO is cited), better information gathering (longitudinal data, targeted questions), better comprehension and adherence (culturally personalised instructions).

## Prerequisites
- **Trust:** a *performance floor* ("do no harm": no missed diagnoses, no harmful treatment, no bias) and a *performance ceiling* (share and quality of clinician tasks covered, including empathy). Adoption will grow regardless, so guardrails, risk controls and accountability matter more.
- **Privacy and security:** trust builds incrementally. Organisations should enable **progressive disclosure**, where patients choose what to share in each interaction as value is demonstrated.
- **Fuller patient context:** "a limiting factor for discursive care is **data availability, rather than model capability**." Clinical records, benefits, utilisation history and preferences need contextualising.
- **Operating models:** consumer AI platforms (agentic actions into EHRs: referrals, prior authorisation, billing), digital-native "AI clinician as a product", health-system in-house AI ("clinical expertise as a service"), and payer in-house AI (claims, enrolment and adjudication connected to an AI clinician).

## What it does NOT cover (useful gaps for the council)
- No low- or middle-income or single-payer perspective (it is entirely US, multi-payer, fee-for-service vs value-based).
- No technical method: it gives no detail on how the performance floor would be *measured* or *certified*.
- Vectors 2 and 3 are only named, not analysed.
- Language, culture and dialect are reduced to "culturally personalized instructions".
- Deskilling is named but no countermeasure is specified.
